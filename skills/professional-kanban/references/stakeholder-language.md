# Stakeholder language

Read this when the user has to take a forecast to someone who does not want a
forecast. The metrics are the easy part; the conversation is where this usually
fails.

## The one move underneath all of these

Every one of these conversations is the same move: **do not defend the method,
answer the question underneath it.** Nobody wants percentiles. They want to know
whether to make a promise, whether to worry, or whether to move money. Give them
that, in their terms, with the range attached — and let the method stay in the
background where it belongs.

An answer that is technically correct and gets ignored has failed.

## "Just give me a date"

The instinct is to explain why single dates are wrong. That reads as evasion and
loses the room. Give a date — with the half of the sentence that makes it honest.

> "October 15. That's the date I'd commit to, and I'd expect to hit it about 17
> times out of 20. If you need something closer to certain, that's October 29. If
> you can carry more risk, September 30 gets you there about half the time. Which
> one do you want to plan against?"

That is still a forecast, but it is shaped like an answer. The person asking now has
a choice to make, which is what they actually came for.

If they push for a single number with no range at all, name the trade in one line:
"I can give you one number, but then you won't know whether it's the safe one or the
optimistic one — and you'd be planning as though it were the safe one."

## "I need 100% certainty"

Do not argue. Show them what 100% costs.

> "The only forecast I can give you at 100% is 'zero items or more' — that's the
> only outcome nothing can break. Every useful number is below 100%. The question
> is which risk level you want to plan at, and that's a business decision rather
> than a data one."

Then offer 85% and 95% as the real options, and be clear that 95% buys its
confidence with a later date rather than with more work.

## "Why is this taking so long?"

The reflex answer blames complexity or individuals. Route to age and WIP instead —
that moves the conversation from blame toward something that can actually change.

> "Four items have been in progress longer than 85% of everything we've finished
> this year. Three of them started before the two that finished last week. The
> pattern isn't that the work got harder — it's that we have more started than we
> can finish, so everything ages while it waits."

Then give the two levers, which are always the same two: finish work, and don't
start work.

If someone asks which person is slow, redirect without moralizing: flow metrics
describe a workflow's health, not a person's. An item aging in review says something
about the review policy, not the reviewer.

## "Team A is faster than Team B"

Throughput counts items, and two teams almost never mean the same thing by "item".
Comparing them measures item size, not speed.

> "Those numbers aren't comparable — they're counting different things. What I can
> tell you is whether each team is getting more predictable over time, which is the
> question that actually helps."

The useful comparison is a team against its own history: is the spread between the
50th and 85th percentile narrowing? That is predictability improving, and it is the
thing worth rewarding.

## "What's our velocity?" / "Can you put this in story points?"

Answer with throughput and name the difference briefly, once.

> "We finished 41 items last month. That's a count of things that reached done —
> no conversion, no inflation, and it's the input the forecast actually uses.
> Points would give us a number that only means something inside this team and
> that drifts every time we re-estimate."

Do not relitigate this every time it comes up. Say it once, give the flow answer,
and move on. Repeating it turns a method preference into a personality trait.

## "Can we just add an expedite lane for this one?"

An expedite lane does not create capacity. It reorders the queue, and everything it
jumps ages further.

> "We can pull it next — but the honest version is that three other items get older
> while it goes through, and two of them are already past our 70th percentile. If
> it's genuinely more important than those, that's a fine trade to make on purpose.
> The problem is when the lane becomes permanent and everything ends up in it."

Control WIP and manage age for everything, rather than building a permanent bypass.

## Turning a forecast into a commitment decision

A forecast is an input to a decision, not the decision. The bridge:

1. **What are you deciding?** A launch date, a hiring case, whether to tell a
   customer. The decision determines which percentile matters.
2. **What does being wrong cost?** A missed internal demo is cheap; a missed
   regulatory deadline is not. Expensive misses justify 95%; cheap ones can run
   at 50%.
3. **Commit at that level, and say the level out loud.** "We're committing at 85%"
   is a sentence a stakeholder can hold you to fairly.
4. **Re-forecast when anything changes.** Scope changed, refinement changed the
   understanding, the team changed — re-run it. Forecasting is not set-and-forget.

## When the forecast is bad news

The temptation is to soften the number. Do not. Soften the delivery, keep the number.

> "At the current rate, the 85% date is three weeks past the one we've been
> planning around. Here's what actually moves it: cutting scope moves it the most,
> reducing what's in progress moves it some, and adding people to this system this
> late probably moves it the wrong way. Which of those do you want to look at?"

Every number should arrive with a lever attached. A forecast that only says "you
will be late" invites the one response that reliably makes things worse, which is
starting more work.
